Least Angle Regression (LAR)

estimation / optimization Section 3.4.4 Path Algorithm Efron, Hastie, Johnstone & Tibshirani (2004)

A “democratic” version of forward stepwise regression: each variable's coefficient advances only until a competitor catches up in correlation with the residual — and one tiny modification makes it compute the entire lasso path.

Benefits & Tradeoffs
Benefits
  • “can be viewed as a kind of ‘democratic’ version of forward stepwise regression” — it enters variables gradually instead of all-in (ESL §3.4.4)1
  • “only enters ‘as much’ of a predictor as it deserves” — a variable's coefficient moves only until a competitor catches up (ESL §3.4.4)
  • “intimately connected with the lasso, and in fact provides an extremely efficient algorithm for computing the entire lasso path” (ESL §3.4.4)
  • “extremely efficient, requiring the same order of computation as that of a single least squares fit using the p predictors” (ESL §3.4.4)
  • Piecewise-linear by construction — “we can work out the exact step length at the beginning of each step” (ESL §3.4.4, Ex. 3.25); no small steps, no rechecking of correlations
  • “after the kth step of the LAR procedure, the effective degrees of freedom of the fit vector is exactly k” — honest model-size accounting (ESL §3.4.4)3
Tradeoffs
  • No sparsity by itself: “Least angle regression always takes p steps to get to the full least squares estimates” (ESL §3.4.4) — every variable eventually enters; you still need a stopping rule (e.g. cross-validation) to pick a point on the path
  • Defined algorithmically, not as the argmin of an explicit criterion: “the LAR algorithm is stated in terms of correlations” (Algorithm 3.2), whereas the lasso is defined by “the lasso criterion (3.52), which we write in vector form” as (3.57) (ESL §3.4.4) — LAR's properties come from analyzing the algorithm itself1
  • The lasso modification “can have more than p steps” — variables can drop out and re-enter (ESL §3.4.4)
  • Requires standardized predictors — mean zero, unit norm (Algorithm 3.2, step 1)
“Least angle regression uses a similar strategy, but only enters ‘as much’ of a predictor as it deserves.” — The Elements of Statistical Learning, §3.4.4, Hastie, Tibshirani & Friedman

The equiangular direction is not an aesthetic choice — it is the unique direction that keeps the tied correlations tied as they decrease (ESL Ex. 3.23; the equal-angle property is Ex. 3.24). Stepwise jumps through intermediate projections — full least-squares refit at each hop, and here greed even picks a different entry order (x₁, x₃, x₂) than LAR's democratic one (x₁, x₂, x₃). ESL: "the more aggressive forward stepwise starts to overfit quite early… and ultimately performs worse than the slower forward stagewise regression" (§3.4.4, Fig. 3.16).1 Stagewise takes "many tiny steps" (Concept 163, 164) — the zigzag is real, alternating crumbs between whichever predictor is currently most correlated. LAR is the compromise the chapter has been building toward: continuous like stagewise, exact and finite like stepwise — p bends, then done. Lasso walks LAR's path until a coefficient hits zero (watch the flash), drops it (rule 4a), and finishes on a corrected route — see Viz 4. Ridge (Concept 147) is reinterpreted by this picture: relax λ from ∞ to 0 and its fit traces a smooth curve from 0 to the same ŷ, bowing toward high-variance directions (ESL Fig. 3.8's profiles, seen in response space). Shrinkage = stopping early along a path; the methods differ in which path — and where they let you stop.

Viz 5 — Something Magical: df After k Steps Is Exactly k
A property, not a process — so a single annotated chart (hover for values). Monte Carlo estimate of effective degrees of freedom, definition (3.60): fixed design (dataset A), true β, σ = 0.75 known, B = 1000 replicate draws of y; error bars ±2 SE from 10 batches. Greedy selection spends more df than it admits; LAR spends exactly one per step.

“Suppose that we carry out a best subset selection to determine the ‘optimal’ set of k predictors. Then the resulting model has k parameters, but in some sense we have used up more than k degrees of freedom” (ESL §3.4.4) — and “there is no closed form method for estimating df($\hat y$) for best subset selection.” Then: “For LAR and lasso, something magical happens. … after the kth step of the LAR procedure, the effective degrees of freedom of the fit vector is exactly k” (ESL §3.4.4).3

Lasso caveat: since the modified procedure can take more than p steps, for the lasso df($\hat y$) only approximately equals the number of nonzero predictors; the approximation “works best at the last model in the sequence that contains k predictors”3. See Concept 157 for definition (3.60) and ESL §7.4–7.6, and Concept 142 for best subset.

Statistical Guarantees
Every claim quoted from ESL or cited.
Terminates at OLS?YES
“After min(N−1, p) steps, we arrive at the full least-squares solution.” If p > N−1, “the LAR algorithm reaches a zero residual solution after N−1 steps” (ESL §3.4.4).
Correlations tied & decreasing?YES
The direction (3.55) does “what is claimed: keep the correlations tied and decreasing” (Ex. 3.23); equivalently $u_k$ “makes the smallest (and equal) angle with each of the predictors in $\mathcal{A}_k$” (Ex. 3.24).
Piecewise linear?YES
“By construction the coefficients in LAR change in a piecewise linear fashion”; exact step lengths computable (Ex. 3.25). General sufficient conditions — quadratic loss + piecewise-linear penalty — in Rosset & Zhu5; see Concept 182.
Exact lasso path?YES
Algorithm 3.2 + 3.2a “is an efficient way of computing the solution to any lasso problem, especially when p ≫ N” (ESL §3.4.4). Homotopy precursor: Osborne et al.2 — Concept 156.
LAR = lasso = FS₀?CONDITIONAL
“If the LAR profiles are monotone non-increasing or non-decreasing … all three methods — LAR, lasso, and FS0 — give identical profiles. If the profiles are not monotone but do not cross the zero axis, then LAR and lasso are identical” (ESL §3.8.1).4 See 163/164.
MSE behavior (Fig 3.16)STABLE
“the more aggressive forward stepwise starts to overfit quite early … and ultimately performs worse than the slower forward stagewise regression. The behavior of LAR and lasso is similar to that of forward stagewise regression” (ESL §3.4.4).
Degrees of freedomdf = k
LAR after k steps: df exactly k. Best subset: more than k, no closed form. Lasso: df ≈ number of nonzero predictors3 — Concept 157.
Computational costCHEAP

Algorithm 3.2 — Least Angle Regression (ESL §3.4.4, verbatim)

  1. Standardize the predictors to have mean zero and unit norm. Start with the residual $r = y - \bar y$, $\beta_1, \beta_2, \ldots, \beta_p = 0$.
  2. Find the predictor $x_j$ most correlated with $r$.
  3. Move $\beta_j$ from 0 towards its least-squares coefficient $\langle x_j, r\rangle$, until some other competitor $x_k$ has as much correlation with the current residual as does $x_j$.
  4. Move $\beta_j$ and $\beta_k$ in the direction defined by their joint least squares coefficient of the current residual on $(x_j, x_k)$, until some other competitor $x_l$ has as much correlation with the current residual.
  5. Continue in this way until all $p$ predictors have been entered. After $\min(N-1, p)$ steps, we arrive at the full least-squares solution.

Algorithm 3.2a — Lasso Modification (verbatim)

4a. “If a non-zero coefficient hits zero, drop its variable from the active set of variables and recompute the current joint least squares direction.”

Direction and profile

$$\delta_k = \left(\mathbf{X}_{\mathcal{A}_k}^T \mathbf{X}_{\mathcal{A}_k}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{\mathcal{A}_k}^T r_k \tag{3.55}$$

The coefficient profile evolves as $\beta_{\mathcal{A}_k}(\alpha) = \beta_{\mathcal{A}_k} + \alpha\cdot\delta_k$; the fit vector as $\hat f_k(\alpha) = \hat f_k + \alpha\cdot u_k$ with $u_k = \mathbf{X}_{\mathcal{A}_k}\delta_k$ the new (equiangular) fit direction.

Tied-correlation condition (LAR active set $\mathcal{A}$)

$$x_j^T(y - X\beta) = \gamma \cdot s_j, \;\; \forall j \in \mathcal{A}; \qquad |x_k^T(y - X\beta)| \le \gamma \;\; \forall k \notin \mathcal{A} \tag{3.56}$$

with $s_j \in \{-1, 1\}$ the sign of the inner product and $\gamma$ the common value.

Lasso criterion and stationarity (active set $\mathcal{B}$)

$$R(\beta) = \tfrac{1}{2}\lVert y - X\beta\rVert_2^2 + \lambda\lVert\beta\rVert_1 \tag{3.57}$$ $$x_j^T(y - X\beta) = \lambda \cdot \operatorname{sign}(\beta_j), \;\; \forall j \in \mathcal{B} \tag{3.58}$$ $$|x_k^T(y - X\beta)| \le \lambda, \;\; \forall k \notin \mathcal{B} \tag{3.59}$$

Comparing (3.58) with (3.56): “they are identical only if the sign of $\beta_j$ matches the sign of the inner product” — which is exactly the Viz 4 event. When an active coefficient passes through zero, (3.58) is violated for that variable and it is kicked out of $\mathcal{B}$.

Effective degrees of freedom

$$\text{df}(\hat y) = \frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \operatorname{Cov}(\hat y_i, y_i) \tag{3.60}$$

The harder we fit to the data, the larger this covariance and hence df($\hat y$). After the $k$th LAR step, df($\hat y$) = $k$ exactly.3

L$_1$ arc-length footnote: for a differentiable curve $\beta(s)$, $\text{TV}(\beta, S) = \int_0^S \lVert\dot\beta(s)\rVert_1\, ds$; for the piecewise-linear LAR profile this is just the sum of L$_1$ norms of the coefficient changes between knots — Concept 181.

Algorithm 3.2b (the FS₀ modification: non-negative least squares on the tied set) lives on Concept 164.
