Forward Stepwise Selection

estimation / selection Section 3.3.2 Greedy Algorithm

Add the single most useful predictor, refit everything, repeat. Geometrically: leap through a sequence of complete projections — each variable taken all the way in, every time.

Benefits & Tradeoffs
Benefits
  • Always computable: "for large p we cannot compute the best subset sequence, but we can always compute the forward stepwise sequence (even when p ≫ N)" (ESL §3.3.2)
  • Lower variance than best subset: "forward stepwise is a more constrained search, and will have lower variance, but perhaps more bias" (ESL §3.3.2)
  • Cheap in practice: "clever updating algorithms can exploit the QR decomposition for the current fit to rapidly establish the next candidate" (ESL §3.3.2, Ex. 3.9) — see QR decomposition
  • In practice it often matches the expensive search: "On the prostate cancer example, best-subset, forward and backward selection all gave exactly the same sequence of terms" (ESL §3.3.2)
Tradeoffs
  • "A greedy algorithm, producing a nested sequence of models… sub-optimal compared to best-subset selection" (ESL §3.3.2) — an early commitment is never revisited
  • Discrete: variables are all-in or all-out, so like all subset selection "it often exhibits high variance, and so doesn't reduce the prediction error of the full model" (ESL §3.4 intro)
  • Post-selection inference is broken: "the standard errors are not valid, since they do not account for the search process" (ESL §3.3.2)
  • Overfits early in hard problems: "the more aggressive forward stepwise starts to overfit quite early… and ultimately performs worse than the slower forward stagewise regression" (ESL §3.4.4, Fig. 3.16)

"Forward-stepwise selection starts with the intercept, and then sequentially adds into the model the predictor that most improves the fit."

— The Elements of Statistical Learning, §3.3.2, Hastie, Tibshirani & Friedman

Statistical Guarantees
  • Nested model sequence: each model contains the previous — coherent for k-indexed comparison and cheap QR updates (ESL §3.3.2).
  • Feasible for p ≫ N, unlike backward stepwise which "can only be used when N > p" (ESL §3.3.2).
  • No global optimality: greedy choices are never revisited; the selected subset of size k can miss the best subset of size k.
  • No valid naive inference: printed standard errors ignore the search; use the bootstrap (ESL §3.3.2, §8.2). This is the general "not accounting for the search process" problem — effective degrees of freedom exceed k (see Concept 157, and the df comparison on the LAR page).
  • Groups: dummy-coded categorical predictors should enter/leave as whole groups "taking proper account of their degrees-of-freedom" (ESL §3.3.2).
Formulas
Reference material — watch the hops first.
Greedy selection at step k (active set A, residual r)

$$ j^\*=\arg\max_{j \notin A}\; \left|\operatorname{corr}(x_j, r)\right| \qquad\text{then}\qquad \hat\beta_{A\cup\{j^\*\}} = \left(X_{A\cup\{j^\*\}}^T X_{A\cup\{j^\*\}}\right)^{-1} X_{A\cup\{j^\*\}}^T y $$

The refit is a complete orthogonal projection: afterwards $X_A^T(y - \hat y_A) = 0$ — the residual is orthogonal to every entered predictor (all gauges at 90°). "Most improves the fit" and "most correlated with the residual" pick the same variable at entry (largest squared univariate correlation with r → largest RSS drop).
